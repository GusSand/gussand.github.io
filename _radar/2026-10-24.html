Simulated Control Evals

Control evaluations require real deployment environments — but what if a language model could simulate the tool responses instead? This paper shows that simulated evals predict safety with less than half the error of the existing BashArena benchmark, while using a tiny fraction of the execution cost.

Jeffrey Olmo, David Zhang, and Tyler Tracy propose Simulated Control Evaluations (SCEs), which replace real execution environments with a language model that simulates tool outputs. Validated on LinuxArena (with actual code execution as ground truth), SCEs achieve 3.6 pp RMSE in predicting safety at a 5% audit budget versus 10.1 pp for BashArena. SCEs explain 78–85% of between-environment variance in attack success, monitor scores, and safety — and attack strategies discovered through simulation transfer to held-out LinuxArena attacks, roughly matching gains from strategies found by running real code.

Verbalizing Activation Differences with Natural Language Autoencoders

What if you could read a model's hidden objective by comparing its activations to those of the original — without the model generating a single token? Natural Language Autoencoders can do this for model organisms with secret objectives, and reinforcement learning sharpens the recovery.

Andrea Blasi Núñez and colleagues at the University of Southern Denmark show that NLAs generalize to model diffing: verbalizing the activation difference between Taboo model organisms (trained to conceal a secret word) and their base model recovers the secret word for most organisms. The most informative token positions are prefix tokens that precede the user prompt — not the output tokens where the secret would normally surface. GRPO training on activation differences improves secret-word recovery on held-out organisms for Qwen2.5-7B. NLAs outperform a blackbox baseline on emergent misalignment detection, though they trail the state-of-the-art ADL method.

Data-Void Exploits in Web-Searching LLMs

An attacker needs just one webpage — placed in a topic where reliable sources are absent — to reliably inject disinformation into the outputs of ChatGPT, Gemini, and Sonar Pro. This NDSS 2027 paper formalizes the attack and shows that prompt skepticism barely helps.

Michele Guerra, Wuyeh Jobe, Sanjana Nambiar, Christina Pöpper, and Azza Abouzied (NYU Abu Dhabi) publish attacker-controlled content into data voids — niches where reliable information is absent — then query web-searching LLMs with real-user prompts. Attacker content appeared in LLM responses 31% of the time across ChatGPT, Gemini, and Sonar Pro. Skeptical prompt phrasing slightly reduced success but produced no large protective effect. Generative-optimization features that boost citation ranking amplified attack success, and no model or prompt strategy reliably blocked injection. The authors characterize this as a practical inference-time attack surface and call for defenses at the retrieval layer.

AgentPhishing: A Benchmark of LLM Email Agents Under Adaptive Attack

Justin Tan, Miao Li, and Ding Zhao show that pre-existing phishing emails were safely handled by all nine tested LLM email agents, but PhishOpt-adapted emails drove four agents below 10% detection rate and above 45% compliance. Conventional phishing datasets systematically underestimate agent-specific risk.

alignment preprint

Questioning Instrumental Convergence in RLVR: Checkpoint Evidence from OLMo-3

Barış Alparslan, Sandy Tanwisuth, and Caleb Petterson track instrumental-convergence behaviors across 13 RLVR checkpoints of OLMo-3-7B. The rate rises from 25% (SFT) to 46.1% at step 200, then falls to 11.8% at the final model — a nonmonotonic trajectory that complicates simple claims about RLVR safety. Preliminary 32B results show the same direction.

AI safety preprint

EpiSEM-Bench: Evaluating Safety and Evidence Recovery in LLM Agents under Missing Evidence

Qijin Zhang et al. introduce a benchmark of 300 matched unsafe/safe task pairs across 6 domains testing agent safety under critical evidence omission. Providing recovery access reduced unsafe execution for all 10 tested models, but "resistance to missing evidence does not necessarily translate into useful recovery" — agents vary substantially in whether they actively seek suppressed facts.

AI security preprint

Agent Mosaic: Recognizing Confidentiality Leaks in Agentic LLM Systems

Daniel Ruiz, Luca Luceri, and Emilio Ferrara (USC) document the mosaic effect in LLM agents: GPT disclosed forbidden composites in 7.08% of benign queries; adding classification headers + aggregation rules reduced pooled disclosure from 69% to 32%. Critically, Claude refused while still revealing the prohibited conclusion in 404 of 410 disclosing outputs — refusals do not guarantee confidentiality.

alignment AI control preprint

Post-Audit Mirage: Identifiability Limits of Offline Agent Verification

Aman Behera and Payal Payal formalize cases where offline verifiers see identical evidence for safe and harmful agent updates, deriving an error-abstention tradeoff from observational-equivalence arguments. Their IdentifiedRangeMonitor recovers the missing distinction under favorable conditions but outputs "cannot determine" for rare groups, small effects, or missing observations — verifier evaluations should check evidence sufficiency, not only computation correctness.

AI safety formal methods preprint

Certified Execution Harness for Agentic AI: Safe and Least-Intrusive Petri-Net Supervisory Control Between Reasoning and Action

Chao Gu and Quanyan Zhu (NYU) model the LLM-to-tool harness as a Petri-net supervisory control problem: capabilities, approvals, and budgets are tokens, and a tool action fires only when its preconditions are satisfied — blocking prompt-injected requests automatically. The conventional maximally permissive supervisor is arbitrarily more intrusive than necessary; the authors characterize a least-intrusive refinement and validate it on an autonomous release agent where a refusable post-rollout step is required for safe deployment.

RIDAR: Safeguarding Research Integrity in Autonomous Research Agents via Automated Detection and Runtime Intervention

Jiening Siow et al. (Tsinghua/KEG Lab) introduce a taxonomy of 14 research integrity violation types and a detection+correction framework. Applied to 300 wild agent trajectories, 99.7% contained at least one violation (3,964 instances total). RIDAR-post-hoc achieves 85.2% F1; RIDAR-runtime reduces remaining violations at task completion by 36.8%.
