The Geometry of Refusal: Why Post-Hoc Safety Is Fragile and Pretraining-Time Safety Persists

Srikanth Malla, Chiho Choi, Joon Hee Choi (Samsung Semiconductor) — arXiv preprint, September 7, 2026

Post-hoc safety training (RLHF, DPO) keeps getting bypassed by jailbreaks and fine-tuning attacks — this paper gives that pattern a precise geometric explanation and shows why pretraining-time safety does not share the same vulnerability. The culprit is where in the Fisher-curvature landscape the safety update lands.

The paper measures each safety update against the curvature of the model's capabilities using the empirical Fisher of a capability loss. Post-hoc safety consistently lands in a suppression regime: the update is nearly orthogonal to the high-curvature capability directions, with its small in-subspace component concentrating on a few sharp directions — a refusal gate placed over intact capabilities, not an erasure of them. Pretraining-time safety modifies the subspace in which capabilities reside, embedding the constraint where it cannot be peeled off by subsequent fine-tuning. The authors trace this geometry across three model families, providing the clearest mechanistic account to date of why data-filtering and pretraining-time interventions produce qualitatively different alignment durability than post-hoc fine-tuning.

When Safety Routing Breaks: Understanding Alignment Fragility under Benign Fine-Tuning

Yitong Guo, Xiaoyi Chen, Siyuan Zhang, Xiaofeng Wang, Haixu Tang — EMNLP 2026 Findings (peer-reviewed)

Even 100 benign fine-tuning examples can collapse safety alignment — this paper explains exactly why using Fisher geometry. The culprit is not gradient conflict (the prior explanation) but selective re-sharpening of a latent output-routing pathway that safety training leaves intact.

The paper proposes a Fisher-geometric account of alignment fragility: safety Fisher is low-rank, and safety training flattens the geometry while preserving a latent output-routing pathway. After as few as 100 benign fine-tuning examples, this pathway is selectively re-sharpened in output-side MLP modules — attack success rate rises sharply while general utility degrades only mildly. LoRA and ASAM mitigate early collapse by suppressing output-side sharpness, but protection weakens at larger fine-tuning scales. The routing view also explains why a handful of safety examples can restore refusal: they re-flatten exactly the components benign fine-tuning had sharpened. Dataset at Hugging Face: LuckyJason77/safety_routing_data.

From Detection to Refusal: Safer LLMs via Circuit-Guided Weight Scaling

Kuan-Lin Chu et al. — EMNLP 2026 Findings (peer-reviewed)

This paper maps out the three-stage neural circuit that turns a harmful prompt into a refusal — Harmful Detection Heads, Safety Neurons, Refusal Heads — and then uses that map to improve safety by directly scaling the circuit's weights rather than fine-tuning the whole model.

Using targeted attention-head and neuron-level interventions, the authors causally validate the three-stage organization: suppressing Harmful Detection Heads reliably disrupts downstream refusal, and directly amplifying Safety Neurons improves safety-relevant outputs without touching other parameters. Circuit-guided weight scaling applied to these components achieves improved safety scores over RLHF/DPO baselines while preserving general capability — an efficient, targeted safety enhancement that complements pretraining-time interventions rather than replacing them.
