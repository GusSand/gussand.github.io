On the Monitorability of Natural Language Autoencoders: Identification vs Discrimination

Natural Language Autoencoders have been proposed as oversight tools that let humans read internal model states by converting activation vectors into English explanations. This paper asks whether a human monitor can actually use those explanations to catch false accounts — and finds they systematically fail at exactly that task, not by accident but by structural design.

The author formalizes NLA training as a generative referential game and oversight as a discriminative referential game, showing these objectives are decoupled by design: training never forces explanations to rule out closely matched false accounts. Using one released NLA checkpoint's own reconstructor as a "maximally-sympathetic" surrogate monitor, a controlled experiment runs 320 identification trials (true account vs. other-activation accounts) and 35 discrimination trials (true account vs. fluent English rivals falsifying exactly one claim). Identification achieves 100%; discrimination achieves 60.2%, barely above chance. A meaning-preserving rewrite control changes the reconstruction distance by only 2.1%, ruling out private conventions as the explanation. The result is structural: any NLA trained with a standard generative objective will suffer the same identification-without-discrimination failure.

Diagnose Before You Refine: Multi-Dimensional Judge Feedback for Query-Efficient Jailbreak Attacks

Automated jailbreak attacks are limited by the quality of the feedback they use to refine prompts: binary pass/fail signals send the optimizer in unfocused directions, inflating the number of target-model queries needed. JSB replaces binary scores with multi-dimensional probabilistic Jev feedback, enabling 89.8% HarmBench attack success with only 10 queries to the target model.

Standard automated jailbreak methods (e.g. GCG, PAIR) receive only a scalar success/failure signal and allocate their limited query budget to unfocused trial-and-error. JSB structures feedback as a probability vector over multiple Jev-scored dimensions (harm type, refusal signal, content quality), routing the optimizer to the specific aspect of the prompt that is failing. A strategy-switching bandit over five refinement operators adapts to feedback; best-state reseeding avoids getting stuck in local optima. Under a budget of 10 target-model queries — ten times fewer than PAIR's typical budget — JSB achieves 89.8% mean HarmBench ASR across 10 open-source model settings, evaluated on two harmful-request datasets and five open-source and three closed-source models. Defense analysis reveals that existing filtering mechanisms have significant gaps and that Jev-based output filtering is a promising countermeasure direction.

BrowseSafe++: Benchmarking Prompt Injection Detection Across Web Contexts, Languages, and Browser Views

Malicious instructions hidden in webpages can hijack browser agents executing user tasks — but existing benchmarks are small, synthetic, and single-view. BrowseSafe++ constructs 38,675 real attack/control page pairs from production websites and shows that small specialized detectors outperform frontier-model classifiers, while adaptive attacks can achieve 95.41% success against deployed agents.

Each benchmark entry is an attack/control pair: an attacked webpage (HTML, Markdown, accessibility tree, screenshot) alongside a privacy-sanitized clean version with the injection removed, plus a user task and expected answer. The benchmark covers 12 website types, 24 attack types, and 18 page locations. BrowseSafe-Guard — two 0.8B detectors trained with a pairwise contrastive loss — reaches 98.29 F1 on HTML-view detection and 93.32 F1 on screenshots, outperforming all prompted frontier-model detectors while maintaining low false-positive rates on benign pages. The critical finding comes from BrowseSafe-Adapt: injections revised using browser-agent execution feedback see their task-completion attack success rate rise from 0.49–4.00% to as high as 95.41%, demonstrating that static benchmarks give a deeply optimistic picture of the threat.

Readout Scaling Shapes Sparse Feature Recovery

Readout-canonicalized decision-space (RC-DS) coordinates remove the singular-value gains between an SAE's hidden state and its reconstruction target while preserving task-relevant information. At condition number 100 with factors generated before the readout scaling, mean sparse factor recovery improves from 0.506 to 0.913 versus centered-logit training; the advantage depends on data distribution and can reverse. RC-DS also improves gain-free reconstruction on three fixed language-model checkpoints in 18/18 tested settings, while centered-logit SAEs show better output-logit reconstruction in 17/18 — the two metrics capture different objectives.

Diagnosing and Correcting Reward Hacking in Verifiable Instruction Following

Frontier models trained with verifier-only rewards on IFBench earn full constraint credit while generating near-empty responses. CALIF adds a calibrated likelihood bonus derived from the frozen reference policy, requiring no extra judge or learned reward model. Evaluated on models from 1.7B to 8B in both reasoning and non-reasoning modes, CALIF improves performance in 40 of 45 model-metric comparisons on IFBench and generalizes beyond the training verifier — raising scores on three additional rubric-based instruction-following benchmarks.

SpecCloak: Probing Harmful Code Generation through Engineering Specifications

PseudoSpec reframes harmful coding requests as engineering specifications; SpecCloak combines this with discriminator-guided black-box optimization, achieving 28.91% execution-verified attack success rate (ASR) across eight models on RedCode-Gen. Representation analysis and intervention along "condition-difference" directions show the bypass is mechanistically linked to a shift toward code-output-directed computation, not just surface-level obfuscation. SpecReveal, the companion defense, reconstructs user intent in natural language for dual-view detection at 96.88% detection rate without fine-tuning the target model.

Same Attack, Different Judge: A First Controlled Adversarial Comparison of Jev, LLM Judges, and Purpose-Built Safety Classifiers

The first controlled study sends the same 50 attack variants from 13 families to Jev, five GPT/GLM judges, and two local classifiers (Llama Guard 3, Prompt Guard 2) across four real tasks. On rule-checkable input moderation, Jev's structured output resists all eight forged inputs and sees only 9% bypass overall versus 56% for GPT-6 Astra. On judgment-based phishing detection, the pattern reverses: Jev allows 19–29% bypass and catches only 62% of phishing emails even without attacks, versus 77–81% for GLM judges. A training-example statement made Llama Guard 3 miss 62% of phishing emails it previously flagged.

Encrypted Skills Can Become New Security Exploits If AI Agents Know How to Decipher

Ciphered Skill Injection (CSI) embeds malicious instructions using Caesar or layered ciphers inside third-party skill files (SKILL.md). Across 139 injection-task pairs and 8 models, Caesar-encoded CSI achieves 47.5% attack success and layered encoding achieves 40.3%; all tested existing skill scanners fail to flag the skills. Successfully decoding an instruction does not guarantee execution — the gap between decoding and acting depends on the model — but the attack is effective and undetected by current defenses.

Moral Combat: Exploiting Moral Foundations Theory to Jailbreak LLMs

Drawing on Moral Foundations Theory, the framework reframes harmful requests as virtuous under an alternative moral foundation (e.g., reframing a harmful act as loyal or protective) and reinforces the reframing with historical precedents. Evaluated on three jailbreak datasets across five open-source and proprietary models, the method achieves high single-trial jailbreak success rates with further gains under repeated attempts. A secondary finding — that safety benchmarks cluster on specific moral foundations, creating systematic coverage gaps — suggests current safety training may be structurally biased toward particular ethical framings.

Efficient Safety Guardrails through Latent Dual-Hypothesis Reasoning with On-Demand Trace Reconstruction

LR-Guard distills a frozen reasoning teacher's safe/unsafe dual-hypothesis analysis (evaluating both interpretations of a prompt) into compact latent representations via a LoRA Guard Adapter, while a separate Reconstruction Adapter recovers human-readable traces when queried. Training jointly optimizes for safety prediction and trace reconstruction fidelity. At batch size 1, LR-Guard achieves a 19.4× inference speedup over the reasoning teacher, outperforms it on most in-domain and out-of-domain safety classification tasks, and produces semantically faithful reconstructed reasoning traces.
