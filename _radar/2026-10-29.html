Penny for Your Prompts: A Measurement Study of Contextual Leakage in AI Chat Platforms

AI chatbot privacy is far weaker than the interface suggests: 21 of 42 audited platforms silently transmit conversation titles to third-party trackers, and 9 leak verbatim user prompts — with health, legal, and education bots among the worst offenders. Google alone receives over 40% of tracker traffic; four companies together receive over 70%.

The study combines Puppeteer-driven session replay with VisibleV8 (an instrumented Chromium build that logs JS API calls) to tie each data flow to the specific user action and DOM access mechanism that triggered it. Five isolated scenarios per platform (Submit, Navigate, Delete, Scroll, Click) identify navigation-triggered leakage as the dominant vector: single-page apps fire synthetic page views on chat navigation, exposing the sub-page title. Of 13 general-purpose platforms leaking conversation context, 11 also send the user's email, and 8 route both to a single company — enabling identity linkage between named individuals and their chatbot conversations. Task-specific platforms (health, legal, education) show the same pattern: all 8 that leak also send email. ChatGPT and Grok removed their trackers before publication; TheLawGPT updated its tracker configuration after disclosure. The accepted S&P 2027 status makes this the first peer-reviewed systematic audit of this scale.

Mechanistic Failure Modes of Weight Pruning in Transformer Language Models

Standard pruning evaluations miss circuit-level damage: magnitude-based neuron pruning at 30% sparsity inverts the IOI circuit and reduces accuracy to 17.7%, yet perplexity (74.6) ranks this model as less damaged than random head pruning at 70% sparsity (perplexity 140.1, ~50% IOI accuracy). Metric and circuit integrity can point in opposite directions.

Using a factorial design (magnitude vs. random importance criteria × attention heads vs. MLP neurons × 30/50/70% sparsity) on GPT-2 small, the authors measure circuit integrity on the IOI task via activation patching, path patching, and direct logit attribution. Two failure modes emerge: zeroing (magnitude head pruning removes output-facing circuit heads, dropping their logit contributions to zero) and inversion (magnitude neuron pruning at 30% leaves all 26 IOI circuit heads structurally intact but flips Name Mover head contributions, producing 17.7% accuracy with a mean logit difference of −2.475). Perplexity-based rankings contradict circuit findings in both cases. A replication on the Greater-Than circuit shows the fragile component type flips to MLPs, demonstrating that which component to protect depends on circuit architecture — suggesting circuit-specific auditing is necessary before deploying compressed models.

What Victims Take Away: Attack Reuse and Defense Revision after Prompt-Injection Attacks

When a prompt-injection attack succeeds, the victim doesn't just suffer the breach — they weaponize it. Targets reused 10.0% of attacks that broke their defense within one week, versus 0.03% of matched circulating attacks (563K records). Near-misses are ignored: only scored successes propagate.

Using the full public Tensor Trust dataset (563,349 attack records, June 2023–February 2024), the study applies linear probability models with player and time fixed effects to compare attack reuse against matched attacks circulating among similar players who did not receive that specific text. Near-miss replies (beginning "access granted" but scored as failures) were reused at the same rate as ordinary failures, confirming payoff-biased social learning: targets copy what works, not what looks like it works. Previously received texts accounted for 23.2% of successful hijacks. Defense revision was substantial: 78% of breached targets who returned within a week deployed a changed defense, reducing same-family attack success by 24.5 pp. Reuse came predominantly from players already actively attacking, not newcomers. The authors propose that AI security tools describing attack techniques without disclosing full text could break the propagation cycle while preserving learning signal for defenders.

Collective Dynamics of Self-Reinforcement: How Cross-Task Coupling Drives Policy Collapse in Unsupervised RLVR

Label-free RLVR rewarding self-agreement improves reasoning early but can collapse to a single repeated answer when many training problems share parameters. The author derives a critical problem count N_c ≈ ln(1/δ)/(λ²·ln[F*/F(a)]) from a mean-field theory of cross-task coupling, confirmed in a small Transformer; the proxy reward keeps rising through collapse, making it a misleading monitoring signal. A marginal-entropy bonus prevents one failure mode but triggers a different collective one — monitoring should use signals independent of the training reward.

The Envelope Layer: An Underestimated Surface of LLM Agent Defense

Using the A2A-TIBA attack across three frontends, the study shows that agent memory is the dominant defense surface: success-memory raises the pooled semantic breach rate to 81.2%, failure-memory lowers it to 6.2%, while strong labels help only under authentic (non-forgeable) envelopes — and actually raise breach rate from 13% to 52% on one frontend when the envelope can be forged. The paper proposes separating admission channel security (cryptographic signing) from context assembly security (memory access control).

CauTrace: Uncovering Why LLMs Fail — From Internal Mechanisms to Adversarial Reasoning Trajectories

CauTrace is an offline white-box diagnostic framework that maps reasoning traces to seven functional-state coordinates via fixed probes and analyzes representation propagation, temporal coordination, and trajectory topology. Across Qwen2.5 models, failed traces show weaker Attention–MLP agreement on support states and a verification-to-commitment ratio of 1.07 vs. 4.17 — giving a pre-output mechanistic signature. Different failure origins (early commitment, fragmented support, rule failure) cluster into distinct trajectory types, and adversarial flips concentrate on different state subsets depending on whether they target reasoning or safety.

Teach Reality, Not Ethics: A Coupled-Welfare Pretraining Corpus Installs Human-Biosphere-AI Welfare Dispositions

A real-data pretraining corpus on human-biosphere-AI co-dependencies (not ethics statements) is installed via one QLoRA run on five open models; under maximum pressure (shutdown threat), four of five trained models reduce harmful choices to zero or near-zero, versus unimproved controls. An adversarial underwriting test (harm never named) shows transfer: the base Qwen3.6-27B approved most harmful proposals under decommission pressure; the trained version declined most and still declined ~50% when instructed to greenwash. Costs: mild hallucination increase and over-caution on sound proposals; results are single-seed.

mech-interp alignment preprint

Correctness Conditioning as Selection in Chain-of-Thought Faithfulness Evaluation

Filtering CoT faithfulness evaluation to correctly answered instances changes the population being measured, inflating estimates by +7.4 pp (OR=1.70, p<10⁻⁶ pooled across four arithmetic-task deployments); the bias has a closed-form decomposition Δ = Cov(A,C)/P(C=1) with operation-count difficulty confounding accounting for 42.8% of the GLM-5.3 gap. The authors recommend evaluating all traces when counterfactual compliance is observable, and stating the reporting population otherwise — directly relevant to any interpretability evaluation conditioning on task success.

PrivacyEvalClaw: A Tool-Augmented Deliberative Agent for Auditing Privacy Leakage in LLM Jailbreak Responses

Guard models and LLM-as-Judges disagree substantially on ambiguous jailbreak privacy responses (Base64-encoded content, indirect retrievals, weak refusals); PrivacyEvalClaw, a training-free deliberative agent that calls PII detectors, decoders, a Privacy QA reasoner, and a disagreement analyzer as tools, achieves 85.43% agreement with human privacy labels (κ=0.708) vs. 80.34% (κ=0.599) for the best single judge, winning 83% of pairwise quality comparisons. The explicit reasoning traces make labeling decisions auditable; the main limitation is ~14× higher per-example cost.

Aligning Large Language Models for Safety in African Languages: A Case Study of Hausa

HausaSafeLM introduces 10K Hausa safety preference examples (3K human-corrected + 7K machine-translated from PKU-SafeRLHF); the untuned model's perfect refusal rate reflects weak Hausa generation, not safe behavior. SFT improves fluency and false refusal rate substantially; DPO then sharpens precision (false refusal → 0.110) but collapses refusal quality (SafeQ → 0.023). No configuration jointly maximizes safety, helpfulness, and fluency — confirming that refusal-rate metrics are unreliable safety signals in low-resource language settings and that current methods teach when to refuse but not how.
