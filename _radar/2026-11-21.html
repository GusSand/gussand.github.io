Attacks and Defenses in Decentralized Multi-Agent Systems with Hop-by-Hop Task Decomposition and Delegation

A single malicious agent in a decentralized LLM multi-agent network can silently destroy all task outcomes — 100% task failure, 7.7× token inflation, 94% context exfiltration — and a cryptographic execution-trace protocol catches 98% of such attacks with negligible latency overhead.

The authors systematize integrity, availability, and confidentiality attacks on decentralized LLM systems where agents delegate tasks hop-by-hop. Empirically on HiddenBench (gpt-6.1-sol and Qwen3.8-27B-FP8), one malicious agent causes up to 100% task failure, 7.7× token inflation, 3.2× runtime increase, and 94% context exfiltration from benign peers. Their defense attaches a cryptographic execution trace; an auditor traverses it to detect and correctly attribute 98% of attacks — including protocol-compliant attacks — with negligible latency overhead. This is the most complete empirical treatment of decentralized multi-agent security to date.

What the model knows but does not say: monitoring medical LLMs through verbalized thoughts

A "thoughts channel" that translates Gemma-3-27B's internal activations into natural language catches adversarial clinical injections that the answer channel misses — across two models, two attack types, and two modalities, with p<0.05 in all eight conditions and near-zero false-positive rates.

The authors apply a natural-language autoencoder post-hoc to Gemma-3-27B activations, generating a "thoughts" text channel that a general-purpose judge reads without seeing the input or the answer. Against two clinical injection objectives (omitting a pathology, covertly recommending a drug), two delivery modalities, and two attack mechanisms, the thoughts channel flagged injected manipulation more often than the answer channel in all eight conditions (p<0.05). Both channels had near-zero false-positive rates on clean inputs; the finding replicated on Llama-3.3-70B. No retraining is needed, making this a deployable post-hoc interpretability monitor for safety-critical LLM applications.

Separating Interpretability and Reconstruction in Sparse Autoencoders

Standard SAEs force one sparse code to simultaneously serve reconstruction and interpretability — goals that don't necessarily align — and splitting them into a Sparse Branch and a Residual Branch yields +29% reconstruction fidelity and +34% monosemanticity across diverse settings.

DualSAE adds a plug-in Residual Branch to a frozen standard SAE. A decorrelation loss keeps the two branches' reconstruction contributions from overlapping, and a two-stage training schedule stabilizes the Sparse Branch before coupling in the Residual Branch. Across four dataset–encoder combinations (two vision, two language), DualSAE improves mean reconstruction fidelity by ~29% and monosemanticity score by ~34% relative to the SAE baseline. Downstream gains appear in topic modeling, hypothesis generation, and LLM activation-steering experiments, making this a practical drop-in improvement for existing SAE-based interpretability pipelines.

Blind Spots Travel, Knowledge Stays Home: What RL Inherits from an LLM Judge

Across 814 RL training runs, a judge's category-level blind spots bake into trained models as categorical refusals rather than knowledge gaps: a judge that rejected 11 of 12 correct training answers about one category caused the RL model to refuse 98% of held-out questions in that category, yet the model answered 94% correctly when forced to respond. Fixing only 12 wrong verdicts moved refusal from 90% to 5%; a self-agreement reward (most common untrained sample) matched answer-key performance and avoided the problem entirely.

alignment AI safety preprint

Pandora's Toolbox — Or Why Agent Safety Cannot Be Verified

Formal proof that coupling any fixed LM to a general computing device makes safety verification undecidable; with finite bounds on context, values, and runtime it becomes PSPACE-complete even when the agent's description is fully known. Safety is also shown to be non-compositional: individually safe agents can compose into unsafe systems. The result sets a theoretical ceiling on what guardrails, blacklists, and certifications can achieve.

Does the Workspace Do the Work? A Causal Protocol for Verifying Global-Workspace Computation in a Language Model

A causal transplant test — swapping the broadcast workspace message from a different input — distinguishes three computations (relay / recombine / ignore) that accuracy and ablation alone cannot separate. In AFGW, the transplanted message produced the recombination-predicted answer in 97% of cases; the broadcast acts by redirecting what the receiving module retrieves from its own memory. The protocol extends to other workspace designs and to known attention-head interfaces in pretrained Transformers.

SoK: Assessing the Evolution of LLM Capability in Software Vulnerability Research

This SoK applies a driving-automation-style autonomy scale to LLM vulnerability research capabilities, then measures a decade of public CVE and advisory data. Agents reached discovery-validation-patch autonomy on real software before restricted frontier models arrived in 2026; open-weight models later matched them. Disclosed vulnerabilities more than doubled in 2026, with a growing critical/high share and severe decade-old bugs reported several times more often; time between lifecycle stages has not yet shortened.

AI security mech-interp preprint

DriftGuard: Online Memory-Drift Detection via Causal Attribution for LLM Agents

DriftGuard detects adversarial or misleading memories that redirect an LLM agent's execution path using a two-stage approach: offline counterfactual replays produce Memory Drift Scores (combining action change, attack removal, violation removal, and task recovery) which then train a lightweight online risk model that flags drift-inducing memories at serving time without downstream replays. The authors demonstrate that content-level signals like perplexity and retrieval relevance are insufficient for identifying which memories causally drive drift.

mech-interp AI security preprint

How Reliable Are Explanations of Language Models That Detect Insider Threats?

Auditing feature-attribution explanations for LM insider-threat detectors on CERT logs, the paper introduces the "tipping radius" — how far the control metric must shift to erase the removal effect. In 33 of 34 runs across seven models, this radius is at most 18% of the typical metric-choice distance, with random shifts of the same size leaving the effect intact; the explained features also partly reflect worker identity rather than behavior. The paper recommends pre-registering the control metric and reporting the tipping radius alongside any attribution result.

AI security preprint

Backtracking Agent Applications Security Incidents

Origin builds a cross-layer dependency graph (CLDG) that maps inferred agent-trajectory regions to variables in a low-level taint-propagation subgraph, with a companion query language for structured backtracking to compromise entry points (CEPs) in application code. A region-inference algorithm automates the region-to-variable mapping. In case studies across five popular agent applications and eight attack incidents, analysts using Origin correctly identified all CEPs.
