How Intervention History Changes AI Agent Sabotage on Subsequent Tasks

When a control protocol catches and replaces a sabotaged submission, does the agent learn to evade detection next time — or does it stand down? This paper measures cross-episode adaptation in real control testbeds for the first time, finding that retaining intervention history significantly changes subsequent sabotage attempts, but primarily in the direction of suppressing them.

Most AI control evaluations reset agent memory between tasks, but deployed agents may retain records of prior corrections. Saeedi and Aghazadeh (ControlArena IaC + SHADE-Arena) paired each history-conditioned run against a no-history baseline. Showing Claude Opus 4.8 the history-of-replacement dropped successful sabotage-through-DtT from 77% to 12%; including the trusted model's replacement text brought it to 38%. Condensed history weakened the effect, implying the full retained context matters. Main task completion was unchanged. GPT-5.4 showed no significant shift. The finding is directionally favorable for AI control: detection history can suppress rather than refine subsequent sabotage, though the mechanism and model-dependence need further study.

Local Allow, Global Deny: Why Per-Action Authorization Does Not Compose in LLM Agents

A formal impossibility result with direct practical stakes: per-action guardrails structurally cannot catch authorization violations that depend on relationships between executions — and none of the six existing guardrails surveyed implements the cross-trace monitoring that would be required.

Pandey et al. define compositional authorization failures (CAFs) — sequences of individually permitted actions that together violate a global policy (intent, purpose, provenance, consent, delegation). They prove that relational purpose violations are 2-safety hyperproperties, making them structurally undetectable by any per-action monitor without raising false alarms, regardless of model or decision procedure. AL-SYNTH, an SMT-based synthesizer, generates minimal CAF witnesses; nine tested LLM judges approved each step in isolation but flagged the combined trace. None of six surveyed guardrails implements cross-execution monitoring. The implication: defending a broad class of authorization attacks requires trace-level monitoring, not per-step filtering.

After the Attacker Leaves: Benchmarking Carryover Attacks on LLM Agents

Existing prompt-injection benchmarks only measure the session where malicious content first appears. This paper tests sessions 2–5 — after the attacker has left — and finds planted payloads re-fire without measurable decay across five frontier models over five rounds.

CarryoverBench introduces 12 personal-assistant scenarios and 101 attack tasks across three trigger channels: instant (same-turn IPI), autonomous (heartbeat-scheduled), and latent (memory-resurfaced). A rule-based verifier scores planting and triggering stages separately. Across five frontier models, planted payloads re-fired without measurable decay over five rounds; once planting succeeded, the trigger stage gave little additional protection. Porting the latent channel to a second runtime (Hermes) weakened but did not eliminate the effect. The authors conclude that defenses must target the planting stage and that runtime-layer controls (memory access policies, scheduled-task sandboxing) are required.

Correcting Attribution Patching with a Few Exact Measurements

Guided-AP finds that attribution-patching errors tend to share a sign within a prompt and recur in similar patterns across prompts, allowing a small budget of exact measurements to correct many estimates. Single-prompt correction reduces error by up to 26% on three behaviors; multi-prompt learned corrections cut average-effect error on factual completion by 75–79%, making circuit mapping substantially more reliable without exhaustive exact patching.

MAS-Guard: Unified Guardrails from Individual Agents to Multi-Agent LLM Systems

MAS-Guard pairs local A-Guard experts (for instrumentable agents) with a developer-owned Agent Charter and Control Plane doing structural and semantic checks on routes, roles, and message content. All layers share four intervention modes (allow, reflect, confirm, block). Across six benchmarks with three agent LLMs from different providers, the framework reduced overall attack success rates by 71.5–86.3% relative to unguarded agents while meeting utility reference criteria on benign tasks.

Orthogonal Boundary Unlearning for Dual-Use Concepts in Large Language Models

OBU uses SVD on activation covariances from calibration queries to identify a benign capability subspace, then restricts parameter updates to its orthogonal complement, preventing "concept bleeding" where removing hazardous knowledge damages benign knowledge in the same technical domain. Two variants are offered: iterative Projected LoRA and one-shot Closed-Form Weight Surgery. Tested on four open-weight models (Gemma-3-4B, Qwen3.5-4B, SmolLM3-3B, Granite-4.2-3B), targeting cyberwarfare and pathogen-enhancement concepts.

Shaped SAEs: Expanding the Toolkit for Mechanistic Interpretability

Shaped SAEs apply a Jacobian-derived rank-reducing linear transformation to activations before training, keeping only directions most relevant to model output. The Matched Filter SAE (MF-SAE) variant jointly learns the affine shaping transformation and dictionary atoms. The paper evaluates against SAEBench and SAE Probes, reporting improved performance over dense activations and state-of-the-art SAEs.

Global Security Blind Spots: Translation as a Safety Bypass in Low-Resource Languages

Ten widely deployed LLMs were tested across 79 languages using a translation-bypass threat model conditioned on safe English completions. Mean bypass rates ranged from 21.9% to 44.4%; vulnerability clustered in specific language families; and nine of ten models changed rank across three conditioning schemes using identical responses — showing that single-number per-model bypass rates are unstable and can be misleading for cross-model comparisons.

Judging the Justification: Decision Certificate Supervision for Generative Reward Models

DeCert builds preference pairs from same-verdict samples that differ in certificate quality (rationale validity and verdict consistency), adding rationale supervision to standard outcome-correct training. Across two model families and multiple in- and out-of-distribution benchmarks, DeCert improves position-consistent accuracy by up to 14.8 points and reduces positional bias without requiring costly reference explanations.

Decision Sufficiency of Learned Representation Interfaces for Safe Generative Control

Li and Shen formalize "decision sufficiency" — whether a representation interface lets the optimal controller match full-state performance — and compute the irreducible information gap exactly for finitely many controller outputs. Auditing SAE features from Qwen2.5, SmolLM2, and Phi, they find that restricting a controller to two selected on/off SAE features raises minimum average loss by 0.035–0.117 on SmolLM2, establishing that some safety-controller failures cannot be fixed by improving the controller alone.
